## SECTION 4: MONITOR LIABILITY CASE LAW (UPDATED 2026-02-23)

### 4.1 Professional Negligence Standards

**NCP Litigation Trust v. KPMG LLP (N.J. 2006)**
- Auditors can be held liable for negligent failure to detect fraud
- Litigation trusts representing shareholders may sue for professional negligence
- Imputation doctrine does not bar claims against professionals for negligent conduct

**Barings Plc v Coopers & Lybrand (2003)**
- Auditors and professionals evaluated against standard of care
- Liability established for failure to detect unauthorized activities
- Precedent for monitor/auditor accountability

**Paragon Finance Plc v D B Thakerar & Co (1998)**
- Professionals required to advise of matters affecting client interests
- Failure to disclose fraudulent activities = professional negligence
- Liability for failure to meet professional obligations

### 4.2 DOJ 2025 Monitor Selection Policy Updates

**DOJ White-Collar Enforcement Priorities (May 2025)**
The DOJ Criminal Division issued new guidance on white-collar enforcement priorities including revised policies on monitor selection:

- Monitors must possess requisite expertise
- Monitors must be independent
- Monitors must provide regular written reports
- Monitors must respond to stakeholder inquiries
- **FAILURE TO MEET THESE REQUIREMENTS = BREACH OF CONTRACT AND POTENTIAL FRAUD**

**DOJ Fraud Section Year-in-Review 2025**
- Expanded enforcement signals increased corporate risk
- Focus on individual bad actors in white-collar crimes
- Heightened scrutiny of professional conduct

### 4.3 False Claims Act Enforcement Trends (2025-2026)

**Cybersecurity-Related FCA Enforcement (Mintz, January 2026)**
- DOJ's Civil Cyber-Fraud Initiative driving major FCA settlements
- Defense contractors, research institutions, and healthcare companies targeted
- **Key principle:** Auditing and monitoring of compliance is essential
- Prompt response to noncompliance required
- Failure to self-disclose discovered failures = enhanced liability

**DynCorp International Settlement (April 2025)**
- $21 million FCA settlement for overbilling State Department
- Demonstrates DOJ commitment to procurement fraud enforcement
- Applies to government contractors including monitors

**Key DOJ Statement (Constantine Cannon, 2025):**
> "Regardless of what other changes the Administration is bringing to regulatory enforcement, DOJ is still committed to prosecuting classic contractor fraud."

### 4.4 Caremark Oversight Duty Developments

**Brewer v. Turner (2025)**
- Board oversight failure doctrine applied to delayed response
- "A board's oversight failure does not always come from what it does not see; often, it comes from how long it waits to act once the warning lights flash red."
- **Applicable to monitors:** Failure to act on known violations = bad faith

**Cybersecurity and the Duty of Oversight (D&O Diary, March 2025)**
- Professor Jennifer Arlen argues oversight liability for "mission critical" risks
- Companies with inadequate systems that caused substantial harm
- Misled customers through false statements about capabilities
- **Monitor Application:** Misleading court about oversight capabilities = liability

### 4.5 Recent Attorney Misconduct Cases (2024-2025)

**Urias v. Daniel P. Buttafuoco & Assoc. (N.Y. 2024)**
- New York Court of Appeals affirmed attorney deceit claims in civil actions
- Attorney liability for deceptive practices harming clients
- Transparency and proper consent required in settlements
- **Precedent for monitor liability to court and protected parties**

**Cleveland Metro. Bar Assn. v. Smith (Ohio 2024)**
- Two-year suspension for misappropriation and trust account violations
- Court-imposed monitoring period as condition of reinstatement
- Demonstrates court's authority to impose oversight requirements

**In re: Todd N. Agron (Mo. 2024)**
- Indefinite suspension for paying unregistered referral service
- False statements during investigation
- 12-month minimum suspension before reinstatement eligible
- **Demonstrates professional consequences for dishonest conduct**

### 4.6 Monitor Immunity Limitations

While DOJ consent decrees typically include immunity provisions, these do NOT protect against:

1. **Fraud on the Court** - Deliberate deception of judicial authority
2. **Breach of Fiduciary Duty** - Accepting payment without performing duties

### 5.1 Fraud on the Court (Inherent Authority)

**Elements:**
1. Court-appointed position (monitor)
2. Acceptance of payment for services
3. Failure to perform required duties

5. Material impact on court's oversight function

**Evidence:**
- $250,000+ paid for services not rendered
- 32+ days unresponsive during active violations
- 1,647 helicopter operations undetected
- Zero compliance reports filed

**Remedy:** Monetary sanctions, disqualification, referral to bar association

### 5.2 Breach of Fiduciary Duty

**Elements:**
1. Fiduciary relationship (court-appointed monitor)
2. Duty of care and loyalty to court and protected parties
3. Breach of duty (failure to monitor)
4. Damages resulting from breach

**Evidence:**
- Monitors accepted appointment with known responsibilities
- Failed to respond to communications for 32+ days
- Failed to detect 166 FAA violations
- Failed to protect disabled individual from harassment

**Damages:**
- Return of all fees paid ($250,000+)
- Compensatory damages for harm caused by oversight failure
- Punitive damages for willful disregard of duties

### 5.3 Professional Negligence / Malpractice

**Standard of Care:**
Independent monitors in DOJ consent decrees are expected to:
- Maintain regular communication with parties
- Conduct systematic compliance audits
- Document and report violations
- Respond promptly to concerns

**Breach:**
- 32+ days unresponsive
- Zero violation reports filed
- Zero compliance audits conducted
- Zero flight log reviews performed

### 5.4 False Claims Act (31 U.S.C. § 3729)

**Applicable If:**

1. Presentment of false claim for payment
