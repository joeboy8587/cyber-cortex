### Claim 1: Fraud on the Court

**Elements:**
1. Monitors appointed by court under DOJ Stipulated Judgment
2. Monitors accepted payment for oversight services
3. Monitors failed to perform oversight duties for 32+ days
4. Monitors remained unresponsive during active violations
5. Court was deprived of accurate compliance reporting

**Case Law Support:**
- *Bullock v. BankChampaign, N.A.* - Defines defalcation in fiduciary context
- *O'Melveny & Myers v. FDIC* - Professional negligence standards
- Court monitors serve as representatives of the court and owe fiduciary duties

### Claim 2: Breach of Fiduciary Duty

**Elements:**
1. Fiduciary relationship established by court appointment
2. Duty to monitor, report, and ensure compliance
3. Breach: 32+ days of non-responsiveness
4. Damages: County funds paid for non-performance

**Key Principle from *Tibble v. Edison International*:**
> "Fiduciaries must act with prudence and diligence... ongoing duty to monitor and remove imprudent investments"

Applied to monitor context: Monitors have continuing duty to monitor compliance and report violations.

### Claim 3: Professional Negligence

**Elements:**
1. Professional relationship (monitor appointment)
2. Standard of care: Reasonable monitoring and reporting
3. Breach: Failure to respond for 32+ days
4. Causation: Violations continued undetected
5. Damages: $250,000+ in wasted payments

**Case Law Support:**
- Professional malpractice requires establishing duty, breach, causation, and damages
- Standard of care for court monitors includes timely reporting and responsiveness

### Claim 4: False Claims Act (If Federal Funding)
